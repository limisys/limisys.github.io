The Mind Has a Boundary

A Research Programme on How Boundaries Become Conditions of Organization That a System Can Identify, Represent, and Maintain

The central question is not simply whether a system has a boundary. It is how a boundary can become part of the system’s own organization: something the system can distinguish, represent within itself, and preserve or revise as its state changes.

Document typeProgramme-level research statement
VersionEnglish working edition · 24 October 2026
ScopePhilosophical premise, formal structures, engineering direction
The programme starts from a philosophical premise, develops formal objects for boundary and self-report, studies the conditions under which they determine one another, and asks whether those constraints can be implemented in an autonomous system.

This document presents the research as reported in the programme overview and its technical supplement. It distinguishes the programme’s starting premise, machine-checked mathematical results, toy-system findings, and engineering goals that remain unimplemented.

1. The central question

A system may be described as having a boundary because an observer can draw a line around it, classify its states, or compute a distinction between what belongs to it and what does not. But an externally assigned boundary is not yet a boundary that plays a role in the system’s own organization.

The programme therefore asks a more demanding question:

How can a boundary become a condition of organization that the system itself can identify, represent, and maintain?

This change in the question matters. It shifts the focus from locating a boundary to understanding the conditions under which a boundary is recognized and sustained within a system. The target is not a universal proof that every autonomous system must have one particular kind of boundary. The immediate target is to define a class of systems, determine the structural properties of that class, and test whether those properties can guide an implementation.

2. The starting premise: self-consciousness and boundary

Programme premise

The core content of self-consciousness is the identification, representation, and maintenance of the boundary between self and non-self.

This is the programme’s starting commitment, not a theorem established by formalization. Lean can verify that a mathematical conclusion follows from definitions and assumptions encoded in a formal system; it cannot, by itself, establish that this philosophical premise is true of human consciousness or of every possible mind.

The premise constrains the questions the programme pursues. Research within this programme should address at least one of three operations: identifying a self/non-self distinction, representing that distinction within the system, or maintaining and updating it as conditions change.

The programme does not need to settle every question about consciousness before beginning. It can instead make its proposed architecture precise, state its conditions, identify counterexamples, and investigate where the architecture succeeds or fails.

3. Three operations, one research problem

The three verbs in the starting premise define three connected research tasks. They should not be treated as three independent modules by default; the programme investigates whether they can be understood as aspects of one organized structure.

OperationQuestion for the systemFormal or engineering focus
IdentifyWhat counts as part of the system, and what does not?A boundary decision or boundary operator.
RepresentHow is that distinction encoded within the system’s own organization?A family of reports and an internal representation of boundary status.
MaintainHow is the distinction preserved or updated when states and inputs change?State transitions, consistency conditions, closure properties, and runtime invariants.

The motivating concern is a mismatch between a system’s actual boundary decision and what it reports about itself. An external log may be stale, inaccurate, or inconsistent with the system’s current state. The programme asks what architectural constraints would make such a mismatch detectable or prevent it from being accepted as a valid state.

4. From an external report to self-representation

A conventional pipeline can calculate a boundary and then emit a report about it. In that arrangement, the report is downstream output: it may be useful, but it can in principle be separated from the state it describes.

The intended constraint is that a boundary-known state without its required self-report cannot be accepted as a valid committed state.

The critical idea is not merely to add a reporting module. It is to constrain the relationship between boundary determination and self-report. The programme distinguishes a weak mathematical relation from a stronger identity claim, and it treats their assumptions as part of the result rather than as dispensable technical details.

At present, the joint-transition diagram describes the engineering target. The technical supplement reports a deterministic toy-machine implementation of a runtime gate, but it explicitly does not claim that the corresponding joint-commit mechanism has been deployed in the production execution path.

5. The formal core: boundary operators and report families

The technical programme represents boundary structure using closure operators. A closure operator expands a state or set to its closure, behaves monotonically, and is idempotent: applying it twice has the same effect as applying it once. Its fixed points are the objects already closed under that operator.

5.1 A boundary generated by interventions

Let Ω be a state space and I a family of interventions or transformations. A set T is invariant under I when every intervention in I maps T into itself. The operator cI is defined as the smallest I-invariant set containing a given set S:

cI(S) = ⋂ { T ⊇ S : T is invariant under every intervention in I }

The formal supplement reports machine-checked results showing that cI is a closure operator and that its fixed points are precisely the I-invariant sets. The boundary structure is therefore generated from a specified family of interventions rather than simply assumed at the outset.

5.2 A boundary induced by reports

A report family R is a collection of subsets of Ω that the system treats as closed or as belonging within a boundary. The programme defines a second closure operator, cR, by intersecting the reported sets that contain S:

cR(S) = ⋂ { T ⊇ S : T ∈ R }

This construction formalizes the direction from representation to boundary: given a family of reports, what closure operator do those reports induce?

5.3 The conditional identity result

The technical supplement reports the following machine-checked result. For upward-closed report families R and R′:

cR = cR′ if and only if R = R′

For an upward-closed report family R, it also reports the corresponding criterion for a specific intervention-generated boundary:

R = Fix(cI) if and only if cR = cI

The significance is conditional. Within the stated class, equality of the induced closure operators corresponds to equality of the report families. This gives a precise mathematical form to a version of the claim that boundary structure and self-report cannot be arbitrarily separated.

5.4 Weak inclusion is not strong identity

The supplement distinguishes the unconditional relation R ⊆ Fix(cR) from the stronger identity claim. The former is reported as machine-checked without the upward-closure premise. The stronger equivalence requires that premise, and its unconditional extension is not supported: the project records finite countermodels and a negative toy-machine criterion.

Interpretive rule: the conditional theorem is not a universal theorem about minds or autonomous agents. It is a result about specified mathematical objects under a stated structural condition. Whether the condition is natural, satisfiable, and appropriate for the intended systems is a further research question.

6.1 The toy-machine result

The engineering experiment uses a deterministic model with three states, interpreted as self, external, and unknown. Seven preregistered criteria are reported: six pass and one—the canonical identity criterion—returns a negative result. This is not presented as a bug to be hidden. It indicates that, in this structure, the fixed-point family generated by the interventions is not upward-closed, so the report family required by the stronger identity condition cannot be found.

The runtime gate was also exercised on the toy machine. The reported number of illegal states rises monotonically from 0 to 1, 2, and 3 as the threshold is varied across the tested settings. This is evidence that the gate can trigger under the toy model’s definitions; it is not evidence that a real autonomous agent already has the proposed organization.

7. The engineering direction

The proposed implementation turns a formal condition into a runtime decision. In the technical design, a state in which the boundary is known but the required self-report is absent is illegal. A state with no boundary decision is routed for review; a state with both a known boundary and a present report may proceed, subject to the design’s consistency checks.

Boundary statusSelf-report statusTarget handling
KnownAbsentReject as an illegal boundary state
Absent or unresolvedAnyReview or request a boundary decision
KnownPresentProceed only after the required consistency checks

The engineering claim must remain calibrated. The toy gate is reported as implemented and tested. A production-level joint-commit mechanism—one that makes the boundary decision and self-report inseparable in the actual execution path—is still a target, not a completed result.

8. Limits and open research questions

The strongest version of this programme will depend not only on its positive results but on how clearly it identifies what those results do not yet establish.

  • The philosophical premise remains a premise. Formalization does not establish that boundary recognition, representation, and maintenance are the essence of self-consciousness.
  • The formal identity result is conditional. The upward-closure assumption must be justified for the report classes that the programme intends to model.
  • A positive witness is needed. The technical supplement identifies the need for a structure in which the stated condition and operator equality hold together, so the strong version is not merely true vacuously for the intended application.
  • Two boundary constructions remain unconnected. The reported identity results concern cI, the intervention-generated operator. The connection to the separately described, core-derived operator cband remains unproved.
  • The bridge to actual systems remains open. Results in finite mathematical models do not yet establish the same organization in biological minds or real autonomous agents.
  • The production implementation remains incomplete. The next engineering step is to integrate the gate into a real execution path and measure both violations prevented and valid states wrongly rejected.

These are not reasons to abandon the programme. They determine what the next research steps must accomplish if the formal architecture is to become an explanatory account of autonomous cognition.

9. A mathematical point that needs resolution

Issue for clarification. In the technical supplement, upward closure is defined by S ∈ R and S ⊆ T implying T ∈ R. The same document presents the threshold family R = {T : for every x ∈ T, w(x) ≥ θ} as an example that automatically satisfies upward closure. Under ordinary subset inclusion, that implication does not generally hold: a superset can add an element whose weight is below θ. As written, the displayed threshold family is generally downward-closed, not upward-closed.

This point does not, by itself, refute the conditional theorem about upward-closed report families. It does affect the proposed motivation for the premise and its applicability to the intended reports. Before publication, the programme should clarify whether a different report semantics or ordering is intended, or whether the family’s definition or inequality needs to be revised.

The toy-machine negative result makes this question especially important. A strong identity theorem can be formally correct and still have limited practical reach if the required premise fails for the report structures the target system naturally generates. Establishing a meaningful class of admissible reports is therefore a central theoretical task, not a minor implementation detail.

10. How to challenge this research programme

This programme should be evaluated by the quality of its definitions, the validity and scope of its formal results, and the evidence connecting those results to autonomous systems—not by the ambition of its title.

Useful challenges include:

  1. Challenge the premise. Is the identification, representation, and maintenance of a self/non-self boundary a plausible account of a core feature of self-consciousness? What counterexample would show that it is incomplete or misdirected?
  2. Challenge the formalization. Are the operators and report families faithful representations of the intended concepts? Are the hypotheses necessary, sufficient, and satisfiable in non-trivial cases?
  3. Challenge the bridge. What argument or experiment would connect the formal boundary to the boundary a real system uses to organize and regulate itself?
  4. Challenge the implementation. Does joint commitment improve consistency in a real execution path? What are the failure rate, false-rejection rate, and performance cost compared with a system whose reports can be updated independently?

A useful criticism should identify the claim under challenge, its assumptions, and the evidence that would count for or against it. A counterexample satisfying all stated premises is particularly valuable; so is a system that appears to meet the proposed functional criteria without the proposed architecture.

The aim is not to make the claim immune to criticism. It is to make the claim precise enough that criticism can help determine what survives.

Questions for the next stage of dialogue

The next stage of this programme can be organized around four questions:

  • What is the most defensible interpretation of “boundary” that preserves the philosophical motivation without confusing it with a merely spatial border?
  • What class of report families makes upward closure natural, and how should reports with graded or noisy readings be represented?
  • Can the intervention-generated operator cI be formally connected to cband, or does a counterexample show that they encode different kinds of boundary?
  • What experiment—potentially involving a feedback-controlled autonomous system—would distinguish a system that merely reports a boundary from one whose boundary and self-report are constrained by a common transition?

These questions are intended as entry points for continued research, not as assumptions whose answers are already known.
